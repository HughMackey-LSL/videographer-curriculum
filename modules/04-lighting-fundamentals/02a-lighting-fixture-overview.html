Lighting Fixture Overview

~15 min read4 objectives

Learning Objectives

  • Recognize the main lighting technologies and common fixture shapes, and describe a useful role for each.
  • Separate the light's technology, physical form, and job in a setup.
  • Read the specifications that affect an interview kit.
  • Choose a fixture and modifier for a small-room interview.

What Are You Looking At?

A fixture is the physical light you put on a stand or mount. “LED” describes its technology; “panel” describes its form; “key” describes its job. A panel can be a key, fill, or background light. A softbox is a modifier attached to a fixture, and a reflector redirects an existing source.

Start with the result you need: a broad soft light on a face, a controlled edge on a shoulder, or a pool of light on the background. Then choose the fixture, modifier, power supply, and support that can produce it in your room.

Four Common Lighting Technologies

LEDs are a practical starting point for a portable interview kit, but film and video crews also work with tungsten, HMI, and cinema fluorescent lights. You may encounter these in a studio, an older institutional kit, or a rental package. Recognizing the technology helps you understand its color, output, power requirements, and controls before you use it.

A color label is not a technology label. A tungsten-balanced LED or fluorescent lamp produces light intended to match tungsten color; it does not contain a tungsten filament. Likewise, “daylight” may describe an HMI, a daylight fluorescent tube, or an LED setting. Halogen bulbs belong to the tungsten incandescent family.

What Is a Ballast?

A ballast supplies and regulates the electrical conditions needed to start and run an arc or fluorescent lamp. It may be a separate box connected to the lamp head, as in many HMI and Kino Flo systems. It is part of the lighting system, not an optional dimmer. Use the specified lamp, ballast, and cables together, and follow that system's operating instructions. LEDs use electronic drivers too, but an external LED power supply should not be mistaken for an interchangeable HMI or fluorescent ballast.

Cinema Fluorescents Versus Office Tubes

Cinema fluorescent systems pair lamps designed for camera color reproduction with suitable ballasts, often operating at high frequency to reduce flicker. They are different from whatever fluorescent tubes happen to be installed in an office ceiling. Still make a recording test at your intended settings. Also check the actual model: Kino Flo now makes LED fixtures as well, and a tube-shaped light may be fluorescent or LED.

These are all continuous-light technologies: they illuminate the scene while the camera records. A photography flash or strobe emits brief bursts and serves a different purpose.

Six Fixture Forms to Recognize

The examples below include portable interview lights and a stage fixture you may encounter when covering performances or events. The shapes overlap across technologies: a Fresnel can use tungsten, HMI, or LED, and a multi-tube bank may be fluorescent or LED. Technology alone does not determine softness; source size, distance, optics, and modifiers still matter.

Stage Lighting: PARs and Gobos

A PAR is useful for covering a stage area or creating a strong wash or beam. For a projected pattern, look for a profile / ellipsoidal spotlight (often called a Leko), a suitable moving-head spot, or a compatible projection attachment.

A gobo is a metal or glass pattern placed in the light's optical path. Projection optics let you focus it into a shape, texture, or logo. On stage it can establish a setting; in video it can suggest window blinds or foliage on a background. A standard PAR lacks those image-focusing optics, so it is generally not the fixture you choose for sharp gobo projection.

Visual references: ETC — stage fixture types and ETC Source Four — profile spotlight and gobo projection.

LED Color Controls

Daylight-only LEDs stay near one nominal color temperature. Bi-color fixtures let you vary the warm-to-cool balance. Full-color fixtures add colored-light modes; some also let you adjust green/magenta tint. The available range and maximum output vary by model, so check the actual controls rather than assuming every LED can match every room.

With tungsten, dimming itself changes color. With cinema fluorescents, changing between tungsten and daylight balance commonly means changing the lamps. With an HMI, the lamp establishes a nominal daylight balance. Matching sources may therefore require lamp selection or gels rather than an onboard color-temperature knob.

Read the Useful Specifications

  • Watts versus lux: watts describe electrical power, not how bright your subject will be. Compare illuminance (lux) at the same distance, color temperature, and modifier or beam setting. A tight reflector can make the center reading look impressive while lighting a much smaller area.
  • Color quality: CRI and TLCI are useful screening measures, but neither guarantees a match between fixtures. Record a face and neutral reference under the actual lights; also look for green/magenta differences.
  • Power and sound: check mains versus battery operation, compatible battery voltage, runtime, cable reach, and cooling-fan sound at the microphone. “Portable” does not necessarily mean battery-powered.
  • Modifiers and support: confirm the mount type, included accessories, stand load rating, and clearance. A large softbox changes both the space required and the load on the stand.
  • Flicker: make a test recording at your intended frame rate, shutter, and dimmer level. Review it for pulsing or bands before the interview.
Why Lights Flicker — and What to Do

Why it happens: light output can pulse with the electricity supply or electronic dimming. Some LEDs use pulse-width modulation (PWM), switching rapidly on and off to control brightness. Your eyes may see steady light, but camera exposures can catch different parts of the cycle, causing brightness or color changes. A rolling shutter can reveal horizontal bands.

Most susceptible: older fluorescents with magnetic ballasts, household or some dimmed LEDs, and HMIs with unsuitable ballast settings. Cinema lights with high-frequency electronics are generally better controlled. Tungsten is usually steadier at normal frame rates, but even it can flicker in slow motion.

  • Test the actual setup: record a few seconds at the intended frame rate, shutter speed, and light level. Play it back; repeat after dimming or switching to slow motion.
  • Adjust the camera: for mains-related flicker, try 1/60 or 1/120 second in 60Hz regions, or 1/50 or 1/100 in 50Hz regions, where the frame rate allows. LED pulse frequencies may differ. Use video-compatible fine shutter adjustment if available; still-photo anti-flicker settings may not apply. Changing shutter also changes motion blur.
  • Adjust the light: try another dimmer level, the fixture's frequency/high-speed mode, or an appropriate HMI ballast mode. If needed, replace or switch off the problem source and use tested video lighting.

References: Sony — flicker and shutter settings; Aputure — LED frequency adjustment; ARRI — high-speed HMI ballasts; ARRI — tungsten flicker at high frame rates.

Example: A Faculty Interview in a Small Office

You have a seated subject, a quiet corner, mains power, and fifteen minutes. A panel with its compatible softbox or a COB LED with a medium softbox could provide the key. Put the luminous face close enough to appear large from the subject's position, just outside the shot. Add a white reflector if the shadows need lifting. Test the fan at the microphone before deciding which kit to use.

If the background needs separation, try changing the subject's position first. Then consider a small accent fixture or a background practical. Adding a powerful second key would solve a different problem and use more floor space.

Before You Turn It On

Secure the fixture and modifier, weight the stand, keep cables out of walkways, and leave ventilation clear. Use manufacturer-approved accessories and power supplies. Household fabric or paper should not be attached to a hot fixture.

Key Takeaways

  • Tungsten, HMI, cinema fluorescent, and LED describe different ways of producing light; fixture form and lighting role are separate choices.
  • Tungsten is typically warm and changes color when dimmed; HMI is typically daylight-balanced; fluorescent color depends on the lamps; LED controls vary by model.
  • HMI and fluorescent systems require compatible lamps and ballasts. Check the actual model rather than identifying technology from a brand or shape alone.
  • A panel or COB LED can be a useful key when paired with an appropriately sized modifier.
  • Compare output under equivalent conditions, and test color, fan noise, and flicker.
  • Plan the whole system: fixture, modifier, support, power, and cables.
